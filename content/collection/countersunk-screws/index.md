A countersunk screw has a flat top and a conical underside that seats in a matching conical recess. Once tightened, the head lies flush with the surface or just below it.

Engineers choose it where nothing may stand proud of the joint: sliding and sealing faces, lids, guide rails, thin covers, and surfaces that air or water flows over. The cone also centers the screw in its hole. That helps location, but it leaves no allowance for holes that do not line up.

## What to check when specifying

- Head angle, which must match the countersink in the part.
- Thickness of the part, so that the countersink does not break through or leave a knife edge.
- Hole position tolerance, because each screw centers itself in its recess.
- Length, which for this head is measured over the whole screw, head included.
- Drive type and size.
- Material grade and finish, including galvanic compatibility with the part. The [material guide](/material-guide) compares the titanium grades.
- Thread lubrication, since titanium threads tend to gall when assembled dry and a seized flush screw is hard to remove.
